01 — THE BASICS

The best prosthesis is not always the one with the most functions. It is the one that best supports a particular person’s life.

BUILDING THE RIGHT FOUNDATION

One device,
many purposes

A prosthesis replaces the appearance or some functions of a missing body part. It may support walking, grasping, balance, sport, or a particular job. It may also be primarily cosmetic. Whether to use one, and which type to use, should be decided with the user—not for them.

01

Cosmetic

Designed around appearance, low weight, and comfort, usually without powered joints. It may also help support or stabilise objects.

  • Strength: quiet and low-maintenance
  • Limitation: little active movement
02

Body-powered

Shoulder, torso, or residual-limb motion pulls a cable to operate a hook or hand. Cable tension can provide indirect feedback.

  • Strength: durable and responsive
  • Limitation: needs larger body movements
03

Myoelectric

Electrodes detect tiny electrical signals from residual muscles. Software converts them into opening, closing, rotation, or grip commands.

  • Strength: broader movement potential
  • Limitation: cost, batteries, training, signal stability
04

Activity-specific

Built for running, cycling, swimming, music, or work tools. “Specialised” does not mean low-tech; it means focused on a task.

  • Strength: reliable task performance
  • Limitation: narrower use case
Not using a prosthesis is also a valid choice

Some people use one only for certain activities; others do not use one at all. A prosthesis is not the only measure of successful rehabilitation. Comfort, independence, participation, and personal goals matter just as much.

FROM INTENT TO ACTION

How does a limb
“listen” to the body?

A myoelectric hand does not read thoughts. It detects muscle activity. The control pathway involves signal acquisition, decoding, movement, and feedback; instability at any stage can affect the final experience.

FROM ACTION TO SENSATION

Grasping is not
the same as feeling

Traditional prostheses mainly solve the outgoing pathway from person to machine. Sensory feedback creates a return pathway: sensors detect contact, then vibration, electrical stimulation, or neural stimulation conveys information to the user.

How should we read the evidence? Small studies show that some participants can use neural feedback to improve force judgement, object recognition, or confidence. Yet “detectable in a laboratory” does not mean “ready for routine daily care.” Sample size, follow-up time, surgical risk, maintenance, cost, and home use still matter.

BEYOND THE DEVICE

The real system
always includes people

Socket comfort, reliability, repair access, and professional support often determine long-term use more strongly than a single laboratory performance score.

01

Assessment & shared decisions

The team considers limb condition, home and school, work, activities, appearance preferences, and budget. Device specifications should not set the goals alone.

02

Fitting & adjustment

The socket connects body and device. Limb volume, sweating, and skin condition change, so a comfortable fit is usually an ongoing process.

03

Training & learning

Occupational or physical therapy builds control and safe, energy-efficient strategies for meaningful daily tasks.

04

Long-term support

Repairs, consumables, software updates, follow-up care, and peer support affect whether a device becomes part of life.

A BETTER QUESTION

Do not ask only, “How many functions does it have?”

  • Is it still comfortable after two hours?
  • Is it reliable at school, home, or work?
  • Are charging, cleaning, and repairs manageable?
  • Are extra features worth more weight and training?
  • Which outcome matters most to the user?

MYTH / FACT

Four common myths

Open each question for an explanation. Good science communication does not pretend that a complex issue has only one answer.

Fact:Advanced systems offer several grips, and research interfaces can return parts of touch or position sensation. A natural hand, however, combines many joints, receptors, and rapid neural adjustments. Current technology restores selected functions; it does not fully copy the hand.

Fact:More features can also add weight, control complexity, and maintenance. A comfortable, reliable device designed for the right task may serve the user’s goals better.

Fact:Fitting, skin care, control training, and device adjustment take time. The body and a person’s needs also change, so rehabilitation is a continuing process.

Fact:Some people value a natural appearance, some prefer visible mechanical design, and others use no prosthesis. Respect means asking about personal preferences instead of defining “complete” for someone else.
